# Holiday dates: where they come from and how they were checked

The holiday calendar in `src/lib/reference/holiday-data/` is generated by
`scripts/build-holidays.mjs` from date-holidays plus the supplements in
`scripts/holiday-supplements/`. This note records how each part's dates are
produced and what they were checked against, so the next person to touch them
knows which dates are firm and which are close calls.

## Overview

The build runs in five steps, for every year from 2026 to 2050:

1. **date-holidays** (3.37.0) gives every country it knows, with each state's
   or region's own days off as `optional` and a note saying where.
2. **The generator corrects the library** before anything is merged: rule
   fixes where a law changed (`RULE_FIXES`), one name for one holiday
   (`NAME_ALIASES`), English names where the library has none or a wrong one
   (`ENGLISH_NAMES`), "Not a day off in ..." on a national day off that some
   regions do not keep (`STATE_GAPS`), and the Chinese, Korean and Vietnamese
   lunisolar calendars recomputed from astronomy, which moves 32 library days
   to the right date.
3. **Supplements drop** library entries that are wrong (`drop` rules, each of
   which must match at least one entry, or the build stops).
4. **Supplements add** what is missing, in file-name order, and an entry with
   the same date and name as a library one replaces it.
5. **Supplements finish**: a supplement's `finish` gets the country's merged
   calendar and returns it reworked, for rules that need every day at once
   (substitute days in Malaysia, Korea's Labour Day substitutes, the US day
   after Thanksgiving under its state names).

Each country becomes one JSON file, loaded on demand (one build chunk per
country). `npm run holidays:check` fails when the committed files are stale.

| --- | --- | --- |

| `india.mjs` | India's Hindu, Jain, Sikh, Buddhist and Islamic calendar | India |

supplement is deterministic and independent of the machine's time zone: the
whole build is byte-identical under UTC, Pacific/Kiritimati,
America/Los_Angeles and Asia/Kathmandu.

### The coverage tests

`src/lib/reference/holiday-coverage.test.ts` reads the committed data (or a
dry run, with `HOLIDAY_DATA_DIR`) and checks the calendar as a whole:

- **Continuity.** A day off listed in two of 2026 to 2028 is listed in every
  year to 2050. A missing year is explained when the holiday appears that year
  under a "substitute day" or "the day following" name, or its fixed day is a
  national day off that year. Names that come and go by design are allowlisted
  with a reason: substitute days; Brazil's, the United States' and the
  Northern Marianas' election days and the US inauguration; Hong Kong's Lunar
  New Year days and day after Mid-Autumn on a Sunday; Taiwan's make-up days;
  Guru Gobind Singh Jayanti in India and Myanmar's Kayin New Year (lunar dates
  that straddle New Year, so some years have two and some none); Sri Lanka's
  Duruthu poya (the January full moon falls on 31 December in 2028 and 2047);
  Mauritius's Assumption and All Saints' Day, which alternate; and Indonesia's
  collective leave, listed only for the years decreed. Five regional
  days that fall on a national one in a given year are listed by year.
- **Frozen lunar dates.** A lunar holiday (a precise list of names: poya, Eid,
  Vesak, Diwali, Mid-Autumn, Chuseok, Lhosar, Tshechu, Chhath and so on) is not
  on one month-day in 2026, 2027 and 2028.
- **Lunisolar fixtures**: Korean New Year and Chuseok and their substitute
  days, 2033's and 2039's corrected days, Hong Kong's 2027 gazette, Taiwan's
  and China's New Year spans, and Vietnam's Tết.
- **Files**: one for every country in `countries.ts` and every ISO code above,
  each with a day off in every year.

All pass on the committed data. Each supplement has its own test file in
`src/lib/reference/` with the published dates it was checked against.

### The gap inventory

The reviews' scan of the data, for day-off names that stop before 2050 and
for lunar-sounding names on one month-day in 2026, 2027 and 2031, found 42 and
65 before this round. After it, 23 names stop early and every one is explained:
one-off or yearly-decreed days (Andorra's parish festivals, Argentina's
tourism days, Bangladesh's 2026 Durga Puja break, Paraguay's football holiday,
Mordovia's Fyodor Ushakov day, Sri Lanka's 2026 "Labour Day and Vesak"),
substitute patterns (Hong Kong's "day following" days, Japan's Citizens'

one) and the lunar cases above. The 54 "frozen" names left are false hits of
the scan's loose pattern ("Holiday" contains "holi", "Valentine" contains
"lent") or solar dates that really are fixed (Nowruz, Songkran, Thingyan,
Pongal, Qingming in years it falls on 5 April).

A rerun of the first half on the current data (day-off names, `public`,
`bank` or `optional`, listed in 2026 to 2028 but not in 2050, substitute and
"day following" days aside) finds 35 names, each explained as above or
added since as a yearly decree or a declared election: China's, Turkmenistan's
and Uzbekistan's 2026 extra days off, Indonesia's collective leave (2026 and
2027) and the 2026 election days in Israel and South Africa.

## The generator: drops, aliases, rule fixes and the lunisolar calendars

`scripts/build-holidays.mjs` does these things between reading
date-holidays and merging the supplements, and runs each supplement's
`finish` last.

### Dropping library entries

A supplement can list `drop` rules (see `scripts/holiday-supplements/README.md`).
Each removes date-holidays entries for its own country, before the merge, and
never another supplement's entries. A rule names the holiday (a string matched
as the merge matches names, or a RegExp tested against the cleaned English
name), and can be limited by years (`from`, `to`, inclusive, default 2026 and
2050), month-days (`dates: ["MM-DD"]`) and note (`note: /Kerala/`). The build
checks every rule's shape, stops if a rule matches no library entry (naming the
file and the rule), and prints how many library entries each supplement
dropped. The loader skips `_*.mjs` (helpers) and `*.test.mjs`.

### One holiday under two names

`NAME_ALIASES` renames a library name before entries are matched, so two
names for one holiday on one day become one entry, and a state's day off joins
the national entry's "Day off in ..." note. A scan of every country for
same-day pairs with similar names found these, which are aliased:

| Country | Library names | Now |
| --- | --- | --- |
| US | "Day after Thanksgiving Day" (federal observance, California) and "Day After Thanksgiving" (7 states) | one "Day After Thanksgiving", day off in 8 states (13 once `library-fixes.mjs` joins the five states that call it something else) |
| US | "General Election Day" (Hawaii) and "Election Day" (10 states) | one "Election Day" |
| VN | "Vietnamese New Year Holidays" (5 days) and "Vietnamese New Year" (day 1) | one "Vietnamese New Year", 5 days |
| RU | Radonitsa under 6 regional names (Irkutsk's is "Unity Day of Remembrance") | one "Day of Remembrance of the Departed (Radonitsa)" |
| RU | Ingushetia's "Eid al-Fitr (Markhash)" and "Eid al-Adha (Ghurba)", against every other republic's "Uraza-Bayram" and "Kurban-Bayram" | one "Uraza-Bayram" and one "Kurban-Bayram" |

Other same-day pairs were left alone because they are different holidays
(Easter Monday and Orthodox Easter Monday, two regions' own days, a
substitute day on New Year's Eve, a festival entry beside the day off it
belongs to). Two pairs were library rule faults, not aliases: Kazakhstan's
Nauryz substitutes are now in `RULE_FIXES` below, and the Cook Islands'
2 January is under "Not fixed".

### Names, notes and regions

- `ENGLISH_NAMES` gives the app's name for a library holiday the library has
  no English for (it falls back to the country's language: Tunisia's Arabic,
  Brazil's and Spain's regional days, Switzerland's cantons) or has wrong
  (typos, mistranslations, the Queen's Birthday under a king). A name is
  looked up without its " (substitute day)" suffix, so its substitutes follow,
  and a one-off library date marked as a substitute gets the suffix too. The
  build summary lists any untranslated library name left over.
- `NOTE_FIXES` corrects typos in the library's notes; `LIBRARY_NOTES` adds a
  note to a library holiday that needs one (Vietnam's Tết days, set each
  year; Russia's names for its Crimea days, which Ukraine and the UN General
  Assembly do not recognise), and `REGION_NAMES` shows Crimea and Sevastopol
  as "Russian-occupied" in "Day off in ..." notes.
- `STATE_GAPS`: in Australia, Switzerland, the United Kingdom, Malaysia and
  St Helena, whose state calendars in date-holidays are reliable for it, a
  national day off that some regions do not keep (no day off that date, and
  not the same holiday moved a few days) gets "Not a day off in ...", and
  becomes `optional` when more than a third of the regions do not keep it
  (Malaysia's Nuzul Al-Quran and Switzerland's Boxing Day become `optional`;
  Easter Monday, not kept in Scotland, and Switzerland's Good Friday stay
  `public` with the note). Elsewhere the state lists are not reliable for
  this (federal days in the United States close federal offices and banks in
  every state), so they are left alone.
- A library `public` day whose note gives it to one group only (women,
  Muslims, the armed forces) becomes `optional`.

### The lunisolar calendars

date-holidays computes the Chinese calendar, and the Korean and Vietnamese
ones (the same rules at UTC+9 and UTC+7), through the date-chinese library.
The generator computes the three again from astronomy and compares:

- Method: GB/T 33661-2017's rules. New moons from Meeus' algorithm
  (astronomia `moonphase`, good to a few seconds), the sun's apparent
  longitude from VSOP87 (astronomia), ΔT from astronomia's table, days at the
  calendar's UTC offset. A month starts on the day of its new moon; the month
  holding the winter solstice is 11; when 13 months start between two months
  11, the first without a principal term is the leap month.
- Leap months computed for 2023 to 2052: 2023 after month 2, 2025 after 6,
  2028 after 5, 2031 after 3, 2033 after 11, 2036 after 6, 2039 after 5, 2042
  after 2, 2044 after 7, 2047 after 5, 2050 after 3, 2052 after 8. The same in
  all three calendars (Vietnam's 2031 leap month starts a day earlier).

Month starts (every month 2025 to 2051, 332 per calendar) agree with
date-holidays everywhere except 2033, where date-holidays puts the leap month
after month 7: its months 8, 9, 10 and 11 start a month late (25 August, 23
September, 23 October and 22 November 2033 are months 7 leap, 8, 9 and 10 in
the library, but 8, 9, 10 and 11). The same in all three calendars; in
Vietnam's the library's leap month is 8 instead. The Vietnamese almanacs
(xemlicham.com, ngaydep.com, baolamdong.vn) give 1 November 2033 as lunar
10/10 and 22 November as 11/1, matching the computed calendar, not the
library's.

A second fault, found by comparing every day 1 to 30 of every month 2025 to
2051 (9,718 lunar days per calendar): in about 20 months per calendar,
date-holidays' days from some point in the month on are a day early (its 15th
on the 14th's date). Its own 1st of the month is right, so the fault is
inside date-chinese's conversion of a date there and back, not in the
astronomy. The months (lunar year, month, first wrong day):

  (6), 2049 m2 (16), 2050 m1 (10), 2050 m5 (18), 2050 m10 (25), 2051 m9 (8).
- Korean: 2025 m3 (7), 2028 m10 (22), 2031 m10 (10), 2031 m11 (21), 2032 m1
  (16), 2033 m5 (4), 2034 m7 (10), 2035 m10 (8), 2036 m11 (27), 2038 m2 (9),
  2039 m4 (13), 2040 m5 (10), 2042 m7 (15), 2043 m8 (24), 2044 m8 (27), 2045 m9
  (24), 2046 m10 (15), 2047 m9 (30), 2048 m10 (13), 2049 m10 (19), 2051 m2 (9),
  2051 m7 (16).
- Vietnamese: 2027 m10 (21), 2030 m11 (12), 2031 m11 (21), 2032 m11 (29), 2034

holiday's date between the two computations, and date-holidays agrees on each:
China's New Year 2027 (new moon 23:56 on 6 February, Beijing time; the Hong

12 of 2034 (new moon 00:03 on 10 January 2035), months starting 2050-02-22
(00:03) and 2051-08-07 (00:05); Vietnam's leap month 3 of 2031 (new moon 23:57
on 21 April). Qingming (the 15° solar term) agrees with date-holidays in every
year 2025 to 2051, with no close calls.

`lunisolarDate(kind, lunarYear, month, day, leap)` is exported for supplements
that need the corrected calendar.

### What is estimated

- Taiwan's make-up days: the rules let the government put each one a working
  day before instead of after; 2026 and 2027 put them after, so the data
  does. Their note says so.
- Vietnam's five Tết days: chosen each year. The data has the Eve and 4 after,
  except 2027, which has the published 4 to 10 February. Their note says so.
- China's swapped working days (调休) after 2026 are decided each year and not
  listed (2026's are, from the State Council's notice). Vietnam's make-up days
  follow the Labour Code's rule (`law-updates.mjs`); the government can move
  them.
- The lunisolar dates themselves are computed, not estimated: the published
  calendars use the same astronomy, and the close calls above are the only
  places a few minutes would matter.

### Not fixed (upstream)

- Cook Islands: 2 January 2034 is both "Day after New Year's Day" and New
  Year's substitute. (Kazakhstan's Nauryz substitute on 23 March, itself a
  Nauryz day, is now fixed in `RULE_FIXES`.)
- Hong Kong 2033: Chung Yeung falls on 1 October, National Day; the ordinance
  has no rule for two holidays on one day, so both are listed on it.

What this section first listed for other slices is done: `festivals.mjs` now
reads `lunisolarDate()`, and `law-updates.mjs` adds Vietnam's second National
Day and Taiwan's 2025 holiday law. The coverage tests are described under
"Coverage" above.

## India

`scripts/holiday-supplements/india.mjs` adds what date-holidays lacks for
India: everything on the Hindu, Jain, Sikh, Buddhist and Islamic calendars,
2026 to 2050. Nothing is looked up. Every date is computed in the file, offline
and deterministically (the output is byte-identical in any time zone), so the
file needs no package and extends to any year.

### Kinds

- `public`: the holidays every central government office in India closes for
  (DoPT's compulsory list), that are not already in date-holidays: Mahavir
  Jayanti, Buddha Purnima, Dussehra, Diwali, Guru Nanak Jayanti, Eid al-Fitr,
  Eid al-Adha, Muharram and Milad-un-Nabi.
- `optional`: days off in some states only, with the note saying where: Holi,
  Ram Navami, Janmashtami, Maha Shivaratri, Ganesh Chaturthi, Raksha Bandhan,
  Makar Sankranti, Pongal and the two days after it, Onam, Chhath Puja, Rath
  Yatra and others. Holi, Ram Navami and Janmashtami are also among the three
  optional holidays Delhi's central offices usually choose, but they are not
  days off in much of the south.
- `festival` and `observance`: Durga Puja (Saptami to Dashami), Navratri,
  Karva Chauth, Dhanteras, Govardhan Puja, Bhai Dooj, Holika Dahan, the start
  of Ramadan and others.

Every lunar entry's note says the date can differ by a day by region. When a
date is a close call, the note names the other day (see below).

### Method

**Astronomy.** The Sun from VSOP87 and the Moon from ELP-2000/82, both as
abridged in Meeus, *Astronomical Algorithms* (2nd ed.), with nutation,
aberration and ΔT (measured to 2027, then Espenak and Meeus's model joined to
it). Sidereal positions use the Lahiri (Chitrapaksha) ayanamsa the Indian
almanacs use. Sunrise and sunset are for the upper limb with standard
refraction. Against astronomy-engine and panchang-ts over 2020 to 2050: Sun
within 2″, Moon within 15″, tithi and nakshatra end times within 21 s,
sankrantis within 12 s, sunrise within 3 s.

**Hindu months.** Amanta months run new moon to new moon and take the name of
the sign the Sun enters during them (Mesha gives Chaitra). A month in which the
Sun enters no sign is adhik, and its festivals wait for the regular month after
it. This finds the known adhik months (Ashvin 2020, Shravana 2023, Jyeshtha
2026, Chaitra 2029). No kshaya month falls in 2020 to 2050. The closest call is
2029: the new moon of 14 April is 48 minutes before Mesha sankranti, which
makes that year's Chaitra adhik. Drik Panchang agrees: Gudi Padwa is on 14
April 2029.

**Which day.** A tithi is the time the Moon takes to gain 12° on the Sun, and
it rarely lines up with a civil day, so each festival is placed by the
classical rule for its worship, evaluated at New Delhi (Kolkata for Durga Puja,
Kochi for Onam, Chennai for Pongal and Puthandu):

| Rule | Festivals |
| --- | --- |
| Tithi at sunrise (the first of two; a tithi that holds no sunrise takes the day it starts) | Mahavir Jayanti, Buddha Purnima, Hanuman Jayanti, Guru Purnima, Guru Nanak Jayanti, Guru Gobind Singh Jayanti, Navratri, Ugadi, Anant Chaturdashi, Chhath, Durga Puja's Saptami, Ashtami, Navami and Vijaya Dashami (at Kolkata, as the Bengali almanacs date them) |
| Tithi at sunrise, the second of two | Rath Yatra |
| Most of madhyahna (the middle fifth of daytime) | Ganesh Chaturthi |
| Last day touching madhyahna | Ram Navami |
| Begun by two fifths of daytime | Vasant Panchami |
| Most of daytime | Govardhan Puja |
| Last day touching aparahna (the fourth fifth of daytime) | Bhai Dooj |
| Aparahna ladder with the Shravana-nakshatra rule | Dussehra |
| Most of pradosh (dusk) | Diwali, Dhanteras |
| Pradosh after Bhadra, with the three-prahar rule | Holika Dahan (Holi is the next day) |
| Nishita (midnight) ladder | Maha Shivaratri |
| Touching arunodaya (the 96 minutes before sunrise) | Naraka Chaturdashi |
| Tithi at moonrise | Karva Chauth |
| Tithi lasts three muhurtas after sunrise, else the day it starts | Raksha Bandhan, Akshaya Tritiya |
| Ashtami at sunrise (Vaishnava, as the gazette uses) | Janmashtami; the note gives the Smarta day when it differs |
| Shravana nakshatra at Kochi sunrise in the solar month Chingam | Onam |
| Sun entering Makara or Mesha before sunset | Makar Sankranti (Lohri the day before), Pongal (and the two days after), Puthandu |
| The civil day the Sun enters Mesha | Vaisakhi |

The ladders that settle a tithi spanning two days follow the classical texts,
tuned until they reproduce every published date below. Two rules were settled
by evidence rather than the texts alone:

- Holika Dahan moves to the second evening when Purnima under Bhadra runs on
  through nearly all of the next day (2026: the gazette's Holi of 4 March), or
  through three quarters of it with a longer Pratipada (2027: the gazette's
  Holi of 23 March).
- The Shravana rule moves Dussehra to the next day only if Dashami lasts three
  muhurtas past that sunrise. Without that condition, 2014 would come out as
  4 October, but India kept 3 October.

**Close calls.** Each lunar festival is recomputed with its tithi moved 15
minutes either way, which is about how far other almanacs' times stray. If that
changes the day, the note says "A close call this year: some almanacs give …".
Sankrantis within half an hour of sunset get the same note. About 40 of the
1,104 entries in 2026 to 2050 carry one.

**Islamic dates.** A month starts the day after the first evening on which the
crescent is "easily visible" from New Delhi by Yallop's criterion
(q ≥ +0.216; NAO Technical Note 69, 1997), since India's committees go by
naked-eye sightings. Months are numbered by counting new moons from 1 Muharram
1447, which India kept on 27 June 2025. When the deciding evening is within 0.1
of the threshold, the note names the day the sighting could also give.

**Notified dates.** `NOTIFIED` in the file holds official dates that differ
from the computation. It has one case so far: Tamil Nadu's government follows
the Vakya almanac, whose Makara sankranti comes some hours later, and kept
Pongal on 15 January 2026 where the modern almanacs say the 14th. In other
years with an afternoon sankranti, the Pongal note warns that Tamil Nadu may
keep it a day later. DoPT publishes each year's list in July of the year
before. When it comes out, add any date that differs to `NOTIFIED` and the test
tables, and rebuild.

Muharram, Milad-un-Nabi and the start of Ramadan), 51 of 54 match. The three
misses were borderline evenings: Muharram 2021 (computed 19 August, kept
20th), and Milad-un-Nabi 2024 and 2025 (computed a day late). Against
panchang-ts, a library tuned to Drik Panchang, every festival agrees for 2020
to 2050 except the cases below (Durga Puja's Navami and Vijaya Dashami were
compared before they moved to Kolkata's sunrise rule, and not since).

Where sources disagree, or where this file differs from someone:

- **Holi 2029**: computed 1 March, with Holika Dahan on 28 February.
  dekhopanchang.com and when-is.com give Holi on 28 February. But at Delhi,
  Purnima only begins at 02:05 on the 28th, so it does not hold the evening of
  the 27th. vedpanchang.com and panchang-ts agree with 1 March.
- **Holi 2030**: computed 20 March. Sources are split between the 19th and the 20th.
- **Dussehra 2028**: computed 28 September, by the Shravana rule. Sources are
  split. The note says some almanacs keep 27 September. Drik Panchang's page
  for 27 September turned out to be computed for Latvia, where the later
  sunrise defeats the three-muhurta test.
- **Raksha Bandhan 2029**: computed 23 August. Sources give the 23rd or 24th.
- **Makar Sankranti**: the almanac day, which is 15 January when the sankranti
  falls after sunset (2027, 2028). DoPT's restricted list keeps 14 January
  every year, and so does Gujarat's Uttarayan, and the note says so.
- **Janmashtami**: the gazette follows the Vaishnava (sunrise) day, which is
  also what most of the north takes off. The Smarta day, which Drik Panchang
  lists first, is a day earlier in about a third of years, and the note gives
  it then.
- **Puthandu 2047 and Onam 2045** differ from panchang-ts. Both are edge
  cases: the sankranti falls just after Chennai's sunset, and Shravana holds
  two Kochi sunrises. These are computed for Chennai and Kochi, where
  panchang-ts was run for Delhi.

### What date-holidays gets wrong for India

`south-asia.mjs` drops these library entries and replaces them: the fixed
Guru Gobind Singh Jayanti on 5 January (the lunar date in `india.mjs` stays),
Kerala's "Vaisakhi" on 21 April (now Vishu, on the first sunrise after the
Mesha sankranti), West Bengal's Bengali New Year fixed on 15 April (now on
the day after the Mesha sankranti, which is the 14th in some years), four
state days on a lunar date that the library pins to their 2025 dates, and two
state days filed under the wrong state. See "South Asia" below.

## South Asia: Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, the Maldives

`scripts/holiday-supplements/south-asia.mjs` fixes the countries next to India
where date-holidays is wrong or has nothing. It computes everything offline and
deterministically, like `india.mjs`, and reuses its calendar: `india.mjs` now
exports `lunarFestivalDays(year, name, place)` (a festival's own tithi rule,
with sunrise and sunset taken at another city) and `udayaDays(year, month,
tithi, place)` (the sunrise rule), and its Naraka Chaturdashi and Janmashtami
rules take a place. India's own dates do not change. Poya days come from
`festivals.mjs`'s `poyaNear()`. Islamic days use date-holidays' own Hijri rules
("1 Shawwal" and so on), moved a day later where a country keeps them a day
after the library, so neighbours line up with the library's dates.

Tests: `src/lib/reference/south-asia-holidays.test.ts` holds every published
date below as a fixture, checks each drop rule against the library, and checks
the structure for every year to 2050.

### Sri Lanka

date-holidays' `LK.yaml` is the 2026 gazette written as fixed month-days, so
every lunar holiday repeats 2026's day in every later year, and "Adhi Poson"
(2026's extra poya) appears every year. The supplement drops these from 2027
and computes them; 2026 keeps the gazette's names and dates (the computation
gives exactly the same 22 days, and its entries replace the library's with
notes added).

Rules:

- **Poya days.** One for each full moon: the date in Sri Lanka on which the
  full-moon tithi begins, if before 18:08, otherwise the next day
  (`festivals.mjs`). Each is named for its lunar month by `india.mjs`'s Lahiri
  rule (the month the Sun enters a sidereal sign in: Mesha is Bak, Vrishabha
  Vesak, ... Dhanu Duruthu), and a month with no sign entry is "Adhi" plus the
  next month's name, as the gazette writes the extra poya of a leap year. A
  year has 12 or 13 poya days; 2028 has two Duruthu poyas (11 January and
  31 December) and 2029 none.
- **Vesak.** "Day following Vesak Full Moon Poya Day" is the day after. When
  Vesak is 1 May (only 2026 to 2050) the day is "Labour Day and Vesak Full
  Moon Poya Day", as the 2026 gazette names it; otherwise Labour Day is its own
  entry (in 2045 it shares 1 May with the day after Vesak).
- **Sinhala and Tamil New Year.** The date (Sri Lanka time) of the Sun's entry
  into sidereal Aries, taken ten minutes before the Lahiri moment (the
  astrologers' published times run a few minutes earlier; that figure is from
  memory, not checked, and matters only near midnight). The day before is the
  "Day before" holiday. 2024 (12 and 13 April) to 2027 all match. Within half an hour of midnight is a close call
  (2040, 2044).
- **Tamil Thai Pongal.** The Tamil almanacs use the Vakya reckoning, whose
  Makara sankranti comes about three hours after the Lahiri one: Pongal is that
  day if the Vakya moment is before sunset at Jaffna, else the next day. The
  lag is pinned by 2026 (Lahiri 15:14 gave 15 January) and 2022 (14:44 gave
  14 January, from memory). Within an hour of sunset is a close call (2030).
- **Mahasivarathri** and **Deepavali**: `india.mjs`'s Maha Shivaratri rule
  (Chaturdashi at midnight) and Naraka Chaturdashi rule (Chaturdashi at dawn),
  at Jaffna. Sri Lanka keeps Deepavali on the Tamil day, Naraka Chaturdashi,
  not on the north Indian Lakshmi Puja (2027: 28 October, a day before
  India's Diwali).
- **Id-Ul-Fitr, Id-Ul-Alha, Milad-Un-Nabi**: date-holidays' "1 Shawwal",
  "10 Dhu al-Hijjah" and "12 Rabi al-awwal" plus one day. Sri Lanka was one day
  after the library in all 12 checks, 2024 to 2027.
- Kind `public` for all (the gazette marks poyas "P, B": public and bank). Poya
  notes say alcohol is not sold.

| Poya dates 2026 (13) | Gazette (date-holidays LK.yaml) | 13 of 13 |

### Bangladesh

date-holidays fixes Buddha's Birthday on 19 May, Krishna Janmashtami on
24 August and Durga Puja on 8 October in every year (2026's true days are
1 May, 4 September and 21 October); its Islamic days are a day early; and it
lists Mujib's Birthday (17 March) and National Mourning Day (15 August), which
the interim government struck off in October 2024 and which are not in the
2025 or 2026 lists. All are dropped and replaced.

Rules:

- **Buddha Purnima**: `india.mjs`'s rule (Purnima at sunrise), at Dhaka.
- **Janmashtami**: `india.mjs`'s Vaishnava rule (Ashtami at sunrise), at Dhaka.
- **Durga Puja (Bijoya Dashami)**: Dashami at sunrise in Dhaka (the Bengali
  almanacs' rule, not India's afternoon rule: 13 October 2024 against India's
  Dussehra on the 12th). **Maha Navami**, the day before, is kind `bank`: an
  executive-order holiday in 2025 and 2026, when offices and banks close.
- **Islamic days**: date-holidays' rules plus one day, as Bangladesh's
  committee usually starts the month a day after Saudi Arabia (11 or 12 of
  12 checks, 2025 and 2026). Shab-e-Barat (15 Shaban) and Shab-e-Qadr
  (27 Ramadan) are new, kind `bank` (executive-order holidays). Eid al-Fitr
  and Eid al-Adha get the day before, Eid and the day after, the core the
  lists have long held; 2026 has the announced breaks (19 to 23 March, 26 to
  31 May).
- **July Mass Uprising Day**, 5 August, public since 2025.
- 2026 also has 22 October, added to the Durga Puja break on 7 October 2026.

## Nepal, Bhutan, Mongolia and Russia's Buddhist regions

`scripts/holiday-supplements/himalaya.mjs` gives Nepal and Bhutan their whole
calendars (date-holidays has neither), gives Mongolia everything but Naadam
(which is in `festivals.mjs`), and carries on the Russian regional days that
the library lists for 2026 only. Two helpers hold the calendars:

- `_tibetan-calendar.mjs`: the Kalachakra lunisolar calendar in its Phugpa
  (Tibetan), Mongolian and Bhutanese versions, from Svante Janson, *Tibetan
  calendar mathematics* (Uppsala, 2007, revised 2014).
- `_nepali-calendar.mjs`: Bikram Sambat and Nepal's lunar calendar, both on
  the Surya Siddhanta, as the Nepal Panchanga Nirnayak Samiti computes them.

Both are deterministic, offline and independent of the time zone (exact
fractions with BigInt for the Tibetan calendar; Julian days and `Date.UTC`
for the rest). `src/lib/reference/himalaya-holidays.test.ts` holds every
published date below as a fixture.

### The Tibetan calendar (Janson)

A month (Y, M) has the true month count n = ⌊(67M′ − β)/65⌋ with
M′ = 12(Y − Y0) + M. When (67M′ − β) mod 65 is 0 or 1 the month number is
doubled: Tibet and Mongolia put the leap month first, Bhutan after the
regular one. Lunar day d of month n ends at the true date
n·m1 + d·m2 + m0 + moon_tab(28·anomaly)/60 − sun_tab(12·(mean Sun − ¼))/60,
whose integer part is the Julian Day Number of the civil day it ends in; a
day takes the number of the lunar day ending in it, so numbers are skipped
or repeated. A holiday on a repeated number takes the first day, on a skipped
one the next day (Paro Tshechu starts on the second of a repeated day, as the
Department of Tourism lists show).

| Phugpa | 806, month 3 | 123 | 2015501 + 4783/5656 | 743/804 | 475/3528 | Kalmykia |
| Mongolian | 1747, month 3 | 172 | 2359237 + 2603/2828 | 397/402 | 1523/1764 | Mongolia, Buryatia, Zabaykalsky Krai, Tuva |
| Bhutanese | 1754, month 3 | 191 | 2361807 + 52/707 | 1/67 | 17/147 | Bhutan |

### Bikram Sambat

A BS month starts with the Sun's entry into a sidereal sign, by the Surya
Siddhanta (mean motions from the Kali epoch, the manda equation with the
epicycle shrinking from 14° to 13°40′), with a 0.01-day epoch shift fitted to
the calendars. An entry between sunrise and sunset in Kathmandu starts the
month that day; at night it is the same day before midnight and the next day
after, except that Shrawan (Karka) keeps the evening before and Magh (Makara)
the morning after, the dharmashastra rules for those two sankrantis.

Checked against the month lengths of the published calendars (as
nepali-date-converter transcribes them; 2083 from the Ministry of Home
Affairs notice): **1,037 of 1,044 months right, 2000 to 2086 BS** (1943 to
2030), and every month of 2081 to 2084. The seven misses are entries within
about 20 minutes of midnight, sunset or sunrise. Modern astronomy does not
fit: Lahiri sankrantis with the best single cut-off hour get 233 of 288
months of 2060 to 2083 right, because the Surya Siddhanta's sankrantis run
from a few minutes to 14 hours after Lahiri's, depending on the month. A BS-dated holiday whose month
starts within 30 minutes of the deciding moment, after the published list,
says so in its note and ends "Solar date, estimated".

### Estimated, and why

- Everything lunar after the published year ends "Lunar date, estimated".
  The Tibetan-calendar versions are exact arithmetic, but monasteries and
  governments can still move a day.
- On the Surya Siddhanta a month's name turns on a sankranti within minutes
  of a new moon in 2028, 2047 and 2048; there the month takes the name modern
  astronomy gives it, rather than inventing a lost (kshaya) month. In 2050 the
  Surya Siddhanta puts the extra month at Shrawan, India's calendar at
  Bhadrapada, so Nepal's Janai Purnima and Janmashtami that year come a month
  after India's.
- BS 2084 on is computed. The converters' tables agree for 2084 and 2085.

### Method

The Hindu dates use india.mjs's calendar (Meeus positions, Lahiri ayanamsa,
amanta months). The rules that decide the civil day are india.mjs's, rerun
with each place's own sunrise, sunset and time zone where the country keeps
local time. Which reckoning each country follows was found by testing both
(India's date and the local one) against its published dates:

- **Deepavali in Singapore and Malaysia**: Naraka Chaturdashi as Tamil
  almanacs keep it (the first day whose last 96 minutes before dawn the
  Chaturdashi touches) in the Tamil solar month of Aippasi, by Singapore or
  Kuala Lumpur time.
- **Thaipusam in Malaysia, Thaipoosam Cavadee in Mauritius**: the full moon
  of the Tamil month Thai (the Sun in sidereal Capricorn) and the Pusam
  (Pushya) nakshatra beside it. The day is the civil day holding most of the
  time Pusam and the full-moon tithi overlap or, if they do not meet, most of
  Pusam; by Kuala Lumpur or Port Louis time.
- **Diwali in Fiji**: the evening (pradosh) that Amavasya covers most, by
  Fiji time, which is how the Sanatan Dharam Pratinidhi Sabha of Fiji set
  2024 and 2025.
- **Diwali in Trinidad and Tobago, Suriname, Guyana and Mauritius**: India's
  date (Lakshmi Puja at New Delhi, from india.mjs).
- **Phagwah in Suriname and Guyana**: the day after the Holika bonfire,
  decided by india.mjs's Holika rule with Paramaribo's or Georgetown's
  evenings.
- **Mauritius's Maha Shivaratree, Ugadi and Ganesh Chaturthi**: India's
  dates, except that Mauritius keeps its Ganesh Chaturthi holiday the day
  after the Chaturthi (2024, 2025 and 2026 all did).
- **Vesak in Singapore**: the 15th of the 4th Chinese month (the Singapore
  Buddhist Federation's rule), from the generator's corrected lunisolar
  calendar.
- **Wesak in Malaysia**: of the Chinese months' 15th days, the one nearest 16
  May at 18:00. Malaysia keeps Vesak in May: when the 4th Chinese month
  starts late it keeps the full moon a month before Singapore (3 May 2015,
  4 May 2023), but it took 31 May over 1 May in 2026. The window this leaves
  is narrow (between 16 May and 17 May 12:00), so the years it differs from
  Singapore (2031, 2034, 2042, 2050) say so in the note.
- **Waisak in Indonesia**: the Jakarta date of the moment of the full moon
  nearest the 15th of the 4th Chinese month. Indonesia's Buddhist councils
  mark the full moon's moment, which is often a day after the Chinese 15th.
- **Nyepi**: Bali's lunar calendar is counted, not observed: 64 lunar days
  to every 63 solar days, so every 63rd day holds two (ngunaratri). The count
  is placed by one constant, fitted to every Nyepi from 2009 to 2026, and the
  fit leaves five settings; the one used also puts a ngunaratri on Anggara
  Paing of the week Sungsang (9 June 2026), which is how the Eka Sungsang ke
  Paing rule in use since 2000 reads. Nyepi is the day after Tilem Kesanga;
  Bali's leap months follow a 19-year cycle, so each year's Tilem Kesanga is
  the Tilem nearest the one 19 years before (the 2009 to 2027 dates anchor
  the cycle). Every result falls between 3 March and 1 April.

Every computed date's note ends "Lunar date, estimated: the official date can
differ by a day." Where another reckoning gives a different day, the note
names it ("Some calendars give 28 October", "India keeps Holi on 23 March").

### Method

**Islamic dates** come from date-holidays' own Hijri rules ("1 Shawwal",
"10 Dhu al-Hijjah", "9 Dhu al-Hijjah", "1 Muharram", "10 Muharram",
"12 Rabi al-awwal", "27 Rajab", "1 Ramadan", "18 Dhu al-Hijjah", "20 Safar"),
evaluated with `new Holidays().setHoliday(rule)`, so the new countries agree
with the neighbours the library covers. Every 2025 and 2026 Eid date found for
these countries fell on the library's day, except Oman's Eid al-Fitr 2025
(31 March, the library has 30 March). Oman matched in 2026, so no country is
shifted. Each Islamic note ends with the library's "Islamic lunar date,
estimated: the official date can differ by a day".

**Eid spans** follow each government's 2024 to 2026 announcements:

**The Solar Hijri calendar** (Iran; Afghanistan uses the same one with the
zodiac month names). A year starts on the day the March equinox falls before
true solar noon in Tehran (51.42° E), else the next day; months 1 to 6 have 31
days, 7 to 11 have 30, the last 29 or 30. The equinox is Meeus's (chapter 27,
via astronomia's `solstice.march`, good to a minute) less ΔT; noon is mean noon
corrected by the equation of time (good to half a minute). Results:

- It matches jalaali-js (Borkowski's algorithm, which date-holidays uses) for
  every year start from 1360 to 1469 (1981 to 2090). The first disagreement is
  1470 (2091), where the equinox falls between noon at Tehran's longitude and
  noon at 52.5° E.
- From 2026 to 2050 the equinox is never within 40 minutes of Tehran's noon
  (2029 is the closest, 40 minutes before), so no Iranian date there is a
  close call. 1404 (2025) was the last one: equinox 12:31 IRST, 20 minutes
  after noon, so 1 Farvardin was 21 March 2025.
- So **the library's Solar Hijri dates were not wrong**: the inventory flagged
  them because 2026, 2027 and 2031 all start on 21 March, but the library does
  move them (Nowruz 20 March in 2028, 22 Bahman 10 February in 2029). The
  supplement replaces them anyway, as asked, and the test proves the 250
  replaced entries are on exactly the library's dates.
- Afghanistan: Kabul's noon is 71 minutes before Tehran's, which would start
  1408 on 21 March 2029, not the 20th. No source says which meridian
  Afghanistan's calendar uses, so the Tehran reckoning is used and the Afghan
  dates of 1408 (March 2029 to March 2030) and 1412 (Kabul within 8 minutes)
  carry "Solar date, estimated: the official date can differ by a day."

**Weekend make-up days**: Azerbaijan (Labour Code art. 105: a holiday on a
Saturday or Sunday, or on the same day as another holiday, moves its day off
to the next working day; a Bayram day inside Novruz is the one that moves, so
2026's Ramazan Bayram, 20 and 21 March, was off on 27 and 30 March),
Tajikistan (single-day holidays on a Saturday or Sunday move to the next
working day; the 2026 list shows 9 March, 11 May and 29 June), Turkmenistan
(a holiday on a Sunday moves to the next working day by presidential decree,
as in 2020 and 2021; 2026 uses the published decree, which also made
28 September an extra day off, and the rule applies from 2027) and Bahrain
(its Eid al-Fitr and Ashura breaks, on a Friday or Saturday). Kyrgyzstan's
2025 Labour Code ended transfers, so none. Uzbekistan's library rule (next
Monday) matches the 2026 decree (Navro'z on Saturday 21 March, off on Monday
23 March).

**Fixed rules**: Novruz is fixed by law in Azerbaijan (20 to 24 March) and
Uzbekistan (21 March), not equinox-based. Qatar's National Sport Day is the
second Tuesday of February (Amiri Decision 80 of 2011). Western and Orthodox
Easter are computed (Lebanon, Palestine, Syria); both are listed every year,
with a note when they coincide.

### Method and rules

**China.** 全国年节及纪念日放假办法 as amended by State Council Decree No. 795
(in force 1 January 2025): New Year's Day, the Spring Festival (New Year's Eve
and days 1 to 3), Qingming, Labour Day (1 and 2 May), Dragon Boat, Mid-Autumn
and National Day (1 to 3 October). Article 6: a holiday on a Saturday or Sunday
is made up on a working day. The supplement gives each such day the first
weekday after it that is not a holiday or another make-up day; a holiday that
shares its day with another one (Mid-Autumn in Golden Week) is made up too, as
the 2020 schedule did. Lunar days come from the generator's `lunisolarDate()`,
so they agree with the library days it ships. Each November the State Council
publishes the next year's schedule, which stretches the Spring Festival,
Labour Day and National Day into longer breaks with weekend working days
swapped in; the notes on those days say so. 2026's schedule (notice of
4 November 2025) is encoded: extra days off as "Spring Festival (extra day
off)" and so on, weekend working days as a "Make-up working day" observance.

**Qingming** is the 15° solar term at UTC+8, which date-holidays computes
("chinese 5-01 solarterm"). The library's dates for China, Hong Kong and Taiwan
match an independent VSOP87 computation (astronomia, with ΔT) in all 25 years.
Closest calls: 2038 (00:29 on 5 April) and 2042 (23:40 on 4 April). The
inventory's "Qingming frozen on 5 April" was a false hit: 2026, 2027 and 2031
are all genuinely 5 April.

**Taiwan.** 紀念日及節日實施條例 (2025): days off on 1 January, the Lunar New
Year (the day before the Eve to day 3; in the generator's RULE_FIXES), 28
February, 4 April, Qingming, 1 May, Dragon Boat, Mid-Autumn, 28 September,
10 October, 25 October and 25 December. A holiday on a Saturday is made up on
the working day before, one on a Sunday on the working day after, skipping
other holidays. When Children's Day and Tomb Sweeping Day share 4 April,
Children's Day moves to the day before (the day after if 4 April is a
Thursday) and then follows the weekend rule.

**Hong Kong.** When Ching Ming (or the day after it, when it is a Sunday) falls
on Good Friday, the day after it or Easter Monday, the next day that is not a
Sunday or a general holiday is a general holiday, as the gazette did in 2010,
2015, 2021 and 2026.

**Ethiopia.** The Ethiopian year starts on 11 September, or on 12 September
after an Ethiopian leap year (in Gregorian years 2023, 2027, 2031 and so on).
So Enkutatash (1 Meskerem) and Meskel (17 Meskerem) are on 12 and 28 September
in those years, and Timkat (11 Tir) is on 20 January in the Gregorian leap year
that follows. Genna is always 7 January: the Church keeps it on 28 Tahsas after
a leap year. date-holidays keys all of this to the Gregorian leap year, which
is a year out for September and wrong for Genna. Labour Day (1 May) and
Patriots' Victory Day (5 May) are two of the four national holidays in
Proclamation No. 1334/2024.

**Mexico.** Ley Federal del Trabajo art. 74 VII as amended (DOF 30 September
2024): 1 October every six years, when a new president takes office.

**Japan.** Act on National Holidays art. 3(3): a weekday between two national
holidays is a holiday. Respect for the Aged Day (third Monday of September)
two days before the autumn equinox makes the day between one. The library's
rule only looks at 22 September.

**South Korea.** The Regulations on Holidays of Government Offices, amended in
April 2026, make 노동절 (1 May) a public holiday. Election days are days off
by law; the Public Official Election Act (art. 34) puts local elections on the
first Wednesday from 30 days before the term ends and National Assembly ones
on the first Wednesday from 50 days before. Only 2026 (3 June) and 2028
(12 April) are listed: snap elections and term changes can move later ones.

**Vietnam.** Labour Code 2019: National Day is 2 September plus 1 or 3
September (art. 112), and a holiday on a weekly rest day is made up on the
next working day (art. 111). The second National Day follows the government's
picks of 2021 to 2026: 3 September when the 2nd is a Monday or Thursday, 1
September when it is a Tuesday, Wednesday, Friday or Saturday, and the Monday
after when it is a Sunday. Vietnamese Culture Day, 24 November, is a paid day
off from 2026 (National Assembly Resolution 28/2026/QH16). Tết is the
library's rule (the Eve and four days after), except 2027, which lists the
seven days the Ministry of Home Affairs published (4 to 10 February).

**Morocco and Egypt.** Islamic dates use date-holidays' own Hijri rules ("2
Shawwal", "11 Dhu al-Hijjah", "13 Rabi al-awwal", "9 Dhu al-Hijjah") through
`new Holidays().setHoliday()`, so they sit next to the library's first days.
Morocco's announced 2026 dates (Eid al-Fitr 20 March, Mawlid 25 August) fall on
the library's days, so nothing is shifted.

**Greece and Cyprus.** Clean Monday is 48 days before Orthodox Easter.
Kataklysmos is the day after Orthodox Pentecost (Easter plus 50).

### Rules

**Weekend substitutes** are one of three kinds, per holiday, as each law says:

- the next weekday that is not already a day off, so Christmas on a Saturday
  and Boxing Day on a Sunday give Monday and Tuesday (Cayman Islands'
  Christmas and Boxing Day, Kiribati, Nauru, Tuvalu, Niue, the Falklands,
  Macau's compensatory rest days, Solomon Islands' Christmas and Thanksgiving);
- Sunday only, to the next day that is not a Sunday or a day off (Papua New
  Guinea's Public Holidays Act 1953; Christmas on a Sunday gives the Tuesday);
- the US rule: a Saturday holiday on the Friday before, a Sunday one on the
  Monday after (Micronesia, the Marshall Islands, Palau, the Northern Mariana
  Islands, the Solomon Islands' other days, and New Year's Day on 31 December
  when 1 January is a Saturday). When that Friday is already a day off (the
  Northern Marianas' Covenant Day on Good Friday's Saturday in 2035 and 2046),
  no extra day is given.

Samoa, Timor-Leste, Tokelau, Pitcairn and North Korea have no weekend
substitutes. Substitutes are named "Holiday (substitute day)".

**The King's official birthday.** The Cayman Islands keep the Monday after the
Saturday the United Kingdom appoints (Public Holidays Act, 2007 Revision); the
Solomon Islands keep the Friday before it (gazettes 2023 to 2025). The UK's
Saturday is estimated as the Saturday from 11 to 17 June: true every year from
2020 to 2026 except the 2022 jubilee. Against the library's gov.ky dates for
2016 to 2026 the Cayman rule matches 8 of 11 (the misses are 2018 and 2019,
when the UK chose the second Saturday, 9 and 8 June, and 2022). "The Monday
after the second Saturday" would match 7 of 11 and miss 2023 and 2024.
2026's Cayman date is the published one. Elsewhere the birthday has a fixed
rule: Papua New Guinea 17 June (by gazette since 2023; a Saturday gives the
Friday, as in 2023), Tuvalu the second Saturday of June, Niue and Pitcairn the
first Monday of June, the Falklands 14 November.

**Lunar dates** (Macau, North Korea) use the generator's own Chinese (UTC+8)
and Korean (UTC+9) calendars through `lunisolarDate` (festivals.mjs's
`chineseDate` now returns the same dates). North Korea has used UTC+9 since
2018, so its Seollal and Chuseok follow the Korean calendar (Seollal 2027 is
7 February, a day after China's). Their notes say "Lunar date, estimated".

**Solar terms** (Macau's Ching Ming and Winter Solstice, North Korea's
Cheongmyeong) use date-holidays' rules `chinese 5-01 solarterm`,
`chinese 22-01 solarterm` and `korean 5-01 solarterm`, as China's and Hong
Kong's Qingming do.

**Islamic dates** (Timor-Leste's Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha) use the library's
Hijri rules "1 Shawwal" and "10 Dhu al-Hijjah", with the library's note.

**Macau's kinds.** The ten obligatory holidays of Law 7/2008 (New Year, three
Lunar New Year days, Ching Ming, Labour Day, the day after Mid-Autumn, National
Day, Chung Yeung, SAR Establishment Day) are "public": a paid day off for
every worker. The other ten public holidays of Executive Order 60/2000 are
"bank": government offices, banks and schools close, many firms stay open. A
public holiday on a Saturday or Sunday gives government staff a compensatory
rest day on the next working day, listed as "optional".

**North Korea's kinds.** Days off are the ones the sources agree on: New Year's
Day, Seollal and Chuseok (one day each), Kim Jong Il's and Kim Il Sung's
birthdays (two days each), May Day, Victory Day, Liberation Day, Foundation Day
of the Republic, Workers' Party Foundation Day and Socialist Constitution Day.
The army days, Women's Day, Children's Union Day, Day of Songun, Youth Day and
Mother's Day are observances; Daeboreum and Cheongmyeong are folk festivals.
Names are the plain English ones, with the official titles in the note.

**Timor-Leste's Lent days.** Ash Wednesday was declared a day off for the public
service every year from 2012 to 2025, and Holy Thursday most years (2013, 2014,
2016 to 2019, 2022 to 2024). Both are "optional", with a note that it is
decided each year.

**Kiribati.** The law's days are kept, plus Labour Day (on the government's
lists from 2025). The government's yearly list often moves a day off to a
nearby Friday or Monday with no legal rule (2026: Gospel Day to Friday 10 July,
Human Rights and Peace Day to Friday 11 December, Youth Day to early August);
the notes say so.

`scripts/holiday-supplements/festivals.mjs` adds 112 festivals and big events
in 41 countries that are not days off, so date-holidays does not carry them:
carnivals, matsuri, lantern and water festivals, pilgrimages, Christmas
markets, and a few music and arts festivals. India is left to `india.mjs`.
`src/lib/reference/festivals.test.ts` holds every check below.

### Kinds and shape

- Every entry is `festival`, except Naadam (Mongolia, 10 to 15 July), which is
  `public`: the Law on Public Holidays makes those days off. Mongolia's other
  days off are in `himalaya.mjs`.
- A festival that runs several days has one entry per day, all with the same
  name, so a trip that starts mid-festival still finds it. The note says when
  it runs ("Runs 18 September to 3 October.").
- Where a rule only gives the core days of a longer event, the note says so
  (Venice: the programme starts a week or two earlier).
- One date-holidays entry is replaced on purpose: Taiwan's Lantern Festival
  becomes a `festival` with a note about Pingxi's sky lanterns (25 entries).

### How the dates are made

Every festival is a rule that works for any year from 2026 to 2050:

- **Fixed dates.** San Fermín (6 to 14 July), the Gion Matsuri, Inti Raymi
  and most Japanese matsuri, which keep the same days every year.
- **Weekday rules.** La Tomatina is the last Wednesday of August; Notting
  Hill is the Sunday and Monday of the August bank holiday; Oktoberfest ends
  on the first Sunday of October or on 3 October, whichever is later, and opens
  15 days before that Sunday; Sechseläuten is the third Monday of April, moved
  to the second in Holy Week and to the fourth on Easter Monday (Zurich city
  council, 1952).
- **Easter.** Every carnival, Holy Week, the Rocío pilgrimage, Corpus Christi
  in Cusco, Merrie Monarch and Vardavar. Western and Orthodox Easter come from
  the standard computus, checked against date-holidays for every year.
- **Chinese calendar.** From the generator's astronomical Chinese calendar
  (`lunisolarDate`, see "The generator" above), which corrects date-holidays'
  2033 leap month (it has Mid-Autumn 2033 on 7 October instead of 8 September)
  and the days its count slips (Mid-Autumn 2039, the Lantern Festival in 2044,
  2045 and 2050). A test checks Mid-Autumn falls between 7 September and
  6 October every year (the range Chinese sources give for this century).
- **Thai lunar calendar.** Loy Krathong and Yi Peng are the full moon of the
  12th Thai month, from the Chulasakarat (Suriyayatra) rules as implemented in
  pythaidate (MIT). The port matches pythaidate for every year 2008 to 2050.
- **Sri Lanka's Nikini full moon**, for the Kandy Esala Perahera: the full
  moon of the lunar month in which the Sun enters sidereal Leo (Lahiri), and
  its poya day is the date the full-moon tithi begins if it begins before
  18:08 Sri Lanka time, otherwise the next day. Moon and Sun from Meeus,
  *Astronomical Algorithms*, self-consistent to about a minute.
- **Balinese Pawukon.** Galungan repeats every 210 days; Kuningan is 10 days
  after.
- **Announced years only.** Eight festivals have organiser-set dates with no
  rule, so they list only announced years: Keukenhof (2026, 2027), Glastonbury
  (2027; 2026 is a fallow year), the Edinburgh Fringe (2026, 2027; the Fringe
  Society is reviewing its dates from 2028), Sapporo Snow Festival (2026,
  2027), Coachella (2026, 2027), Carnaval de Québec (2026, 2027), the Sydney
  Mardi Gras Parade (2026, 2027) and Vivid Sydney (2026, 2027).

Calendar checks:

- **Loy Krathong**: 12 of the 14 dates the Tourism Authority of Thailand and
  others announced for 2014 to 2027 (86%). 2014 and 2015 come out a day late:
  the government's calendar put that cycle's extra day in a different year.
  Every year 2016 to 2027 matches, and the note says a computed date can be a
  day off.
- **Sri Lankan poya days**: all 13 gazetted for 2026, and the Nikini poya of
  2022 to 2026, the last night of each year's Perahera. The 18:08 cut-off sits
  between two close calls (2 March 2026, tithi from 17:56; 28 July 2026, from
  18:19), so a future year can be a day out; the note says the Temple of the
  Tooth sets the dates.
- **Easter**: Western and Orthodox for every year 2026 to 2050, against
  date-holidays.

### Left out

Dropped because their dates could not be validated, or follow no rule:

- No rule, or the organiser broke the pattern: Feria de Abril (2028 is a week
  after Easter instead of two; 2027 not yet official), Chiang Mai Flower
  Festival (2026 was the second weekend, not the first), Surin Elephant
  Round-up, MassKara, Gran Poder (moved to August in 2026), the Cape Town
  Minstrel Carnival (5 January in 2026, not the 2nd), Vendimia, Sturgis,
  the Torch Festival (2026's main night was not the lunar date).
- Only one year confirmed before the shared web-search budget ran out: Hajj,
  Alasitas, Malta Carnival, Toronto's Caribbean Carnival parade, Ogoh-ogoh
  (sources disagree on Nyepi 2027), Strasbourg's and Vienna's Christmas
  markets.
- Not yet announced: Tomorrowland 2027.
- That Luang (Vientiane) now ships in `theravada.mjs`, with Laos's public
  holidays.

### What date-holidays gets wrong, found while checking

All of these are now fixed:

- **Sri Lanka**: every poya day, both Eids, Deepavali and Mahasivarathri are
  fixed month-days in `LK.yaml`, correct for 2026 only. `south-asia.mjs` drops
  them from 2027 and computes them.
- **Chinese and Korean calendars in 2033**: the leap month is in the wrong
  place, so September to December holidays are a month late. The generator
  recomputes the calendars and moves the library's days.
- **Ethiopia**: Timkat is the 20th of January in Gregorian leap years, and
  Genna, Enkutatash and Meskel were tied to the wrong leap year:
  `law-updates.mjs`.
- **Bangladesh**: Buddha's Birthday and Janmashtami were fixed (19 May,
  24 August): `south-asia.mjs`.
- **Thailand**: Makha Bucha, Visakha Bucha, Asalha Puja and Buddhist Lent were
  only there for 2026: `theravada.mjs`.
- **Missing countries**: Mongolia, Nepal and Bhutan (`himalaya.mjs`), Laos,
  Myanmar and Cambodia (`theravada.mjs`), and the rest listed under
  "Coverage".

## How the app shows the calendar

The data says what is on each day. These rules decide where it shows. They
live in `src/lib/trip-calendar.ts` (timeline and trip screen) and
`src/lib/advisor/calendar.ts` (Discover), and change no date.

### Which countries

`calendarCountries(trip, items)` gives the trip's destinations
(`destinationCountries`, which leaves the home country out), plus the home
country when the trip itself names it as a destination or a leg (a week in
London, then Paris). A trip at home, with no other destination, also gets the
home country when an item's place or arrival place names it (a weekend in the
Lake District from London); a trip abroad does not get it from a place alone,
since its outbound flight leaves from home. Each country's days show only on
the days the trip is there: its leg, or the trip's dates when it has no legs.
A trip with nothing to go on gets none. The timeline, Discover, the trip
view's loading and Discover's own retry all use it. The public-holiday check
does not: it still leaves the home country out, since a traveller knows their
own days off.

### The timeline

- A day with nothing planned is added only for a day off for everyone
  (`public` or `bank`). A festival, a day off for some (`optional`) and an
  observance show only on days that are there already: days with plans, and
  days off.
- A festival or an observance that runs several days (one entry a day in the
  data) shows once, on the first shown day inside its run. The run is
  followed in the data past the trip's dates, so a run that started before
  the trip, or a planned day before the trip inside it, counts. Its note says
  when it runs. Of the 1,669 festival runs over a day, 1,644 already say "Runs
  X to Y"; Bhutan's Paro Tshechu and the 175 observance runs from
  date-holidays do not, so the app adds it in the same words ("Runs 6 to 8
  May").
- A day off, and a day off for some, shows on each of its days, since each
  day changes what is open.
- Keukenhof (53 days) is one line on a month in the Netherlands; Germany's
  Christmas markets (29 days in 2026) are one line on a December trip.

### The trip screen with nothing scheduled

The empty state and its Add action stay. Under them, when the trip's dates
are settled and hold a day off, the timeline's day rows show those days,
headed "Days off on your dates".

### Discover

- Festivals & shows is sent to `/api/advise` like any other interest. The
  search finds concerts, theatre and opera, which the calendar never has, and
  a request costs the same either way. The cache key is the destination and
  every interest, sorted, the format it always had.
- Beside the search's Festivals & shows, a "From Manifest's calendar" section
  lists the calendar's festivals on the trip's dates, and its days off for
  everyone (Diwali, Eid and Christmas are days off in many countries), each
  labelled with its kind. It shows when the dates are settled, every
  country's calendar is in, and Festivals & shows is picked. A day off alone
  is enough for it, since many of the biggest festivals (Songkran, Chinese
  New Year, Thingyan, Carnival) are stored as days off. Observances and days
  off for some are never listed in it.
- Each card names its country when the trip has more than one, or when the
  Where field holds a place other than the trip's own destination (the
  calendar follows the trip, not the field).
- While a country's calendar is still loading, a status line says so; if its
  fetch has finished and the calendar is still not in (offline, with the
  chunk not cached), the line says it loads when the device is back online.
- It shows before any search, after one, and when the search fails. When the
  route answers 503 (nothing it could verify, as without an API key) and the
  calendar has listed something, a note says the search found nothing and
  what is below comes from the calendar, in place of the error; any other
  error shows above the section and does not replace it.

### Loading

The trip view loads each country's chunk when the trip's countries change,
and again each time the device comes back online, since a chunk that failed
offline stays unloaded until asked for again.

## Known limits and what is estimated

Notes tell travellers which dates are estimates. In summary:

- **Islamic dates** everywhere are date-holidays' Umm al-Qura dates, moved a
  day where a country is consistently a day later (Sri Lanka, Bangladesh,
  Nepal, Myanmar's Eid al-Adha), or replaced by a published date (India's
  notified dates, Indonesia's 2026 Idul Fitri, South-east Asia's 2027 lists,
  Iran's 1405 calendar). They carry "Islamic lunar date, estimated: the
  official date can differ by a day". India's are its own, from crescent
  visibility.
- **Lunar and lunisolar dates** after the last published year carry "Lunar
  date, estimated: the official date can differ by a day." The Chinese, Korean,
  Vietnamese and Tibetan calendars are exact arithmetic or astronomy, so their
  risk is a government moving a day, not the calendar. The Hindu, Thai,
  Myanmar, Nepali and Balinese dates follow each almanac's rule and name the
  other day in a close call.
- **Spans a government sets each year** are modelled by their usual core and
  say so: Vietnam's Tết (except 2027, which is published), China's bridged
  breaks and swapped working days after 2026, the Gulf states' Eid breaks,
  Myanmar's Thingyan office holidays after 2026, Bangladesh's Eid breaks after
  2026, Oman's moved days, Turkmenistan's moved days after 2026, and the
  government-picked days in Kiribati, Fiji (Girmit Day, Ratu Sukuna Day),
  Tuva and North Ossetia. Indonesia's cuti bersama and Uzbekistan's decree
  days are listed only for the years decreed (2026 and 2027; 2026).
- **Not modelled**: holidays kept only in Sanaa (Yemen) or Iraq's Kurdistan
  Region; Malaysian states with a Friday and Saturday weekend, whose
  replacement days differ (the note warns); one-off days (elections other than
  those listed, state funerals, special bank holidays); school holidays.
- **Library faults left as they are**: the Cook Islands (2 January 2034 is
  both "Day after New Year's Day" and New Year's substitute) and Hong Kong
  2033 (Chung Yeung falls on National Day, 1 October; the ordinance has no
  rule for that, so both are listed on one day).
- **Kinds left for the owner to decide**: Bangladesh's executive-order days
  (Shab-e-Barat, Shab-e-Qadr, Maha Navami, 22 October 2026) are `bank`;
  Nepal's three Lhosars are `public`, as the 2083 notice lists them for
  everyone; Iran's Solar Hijri days are dropped and recomputed although the
  library's dates were right (the notes are the gain); the Philippines'
  special non-working days stay `optional`; Mordovia's one-off Fyodor Ushakov
  day stays.
- **Not added**: Thailand's Labour Day; Lao Khao Phansa and Visakha Bousa;
  minor Nepali community days; Maldives Martyrs' Day; Cyprus's Easter Tuesday;
  Korean election days after 2028; the Falklands' and Macau's half days and
  year-end closures; Iran's Islamic days after its 1405 calendar (from March
  2027), which stay on the library's Umm al-Qura dates until the next
  calendar is added.

## Maintenance: yearly decrees to add

Governments publish next year's days on a rough calendar. When one comes out,
compare it with the data (`node scripts/build-holidays.mjs --out /tmp/h` and
read that country's file), add any date that differs to the supplement named,
put it in that supplement's test as a fixture, and rebuild.
